Aerospace & Flight

I've always looked at aircraft, spacecraft, and launch vehicles the same way I look at complex engineering systems: reduce them down to fundamentals, understand the constraints, and study why each design choice exists. These platforms stand out because they reward deep curiosity and disciplined analysis. They are engineering pushed toward the limit.

Aircraft

Experimental | Fighters | Recon | Bombers
X-15Experimental

Flown by those who went first.

The X-15 was built to go first. Neil Armstrong flew it before he walked on the moon. Early test pilots took this rocket plane to the edge of the atmosphere to learn heat, speed, and control where theory ran out, then brought the data home.

F-117 NighthawkStealth Attack

First in line for pure stealth engineering.

The F-117 was built around one hard constraint: survivability through signature reduction. Accept the aerodynamic tradeoffs, then use systems and mission design to make the aircraft effective anyway.

SR-71 BlackbirdRecon | Mach 3+

Outrun the threat instead of dodging it.

I'm drawn to how the SR-71 handles continuous heating, material expansion, and mission reliability at Mach 3+. At that regime every design choice hits survivability and performance directly.

F-22 Raptor5th Gen Fighter

Stealth, supercruise, and control laws.

On the F-22, aerodynamics, avionics, and control laws work as one system. Real-time software, stealth geometry, and thrust vectoring read as integrated, not bolted on.

YF-23 "Black Widow II"Prototype

The stealthy "what if" path.

The YF-23 is the other ATF path: signature reduction, high-speed stability, and a planform built around different priorities than the F-22. Same problem, different solution.

F-16 "Viper"FBW Pioneer

Relaxed stability as a feature.

The F-16's intentionally unstable design has always been one of my favorite examples of control theory in action. It shows how software, real-time feedback, and control surface authority can turn instability into agility.

B-2 SpiritStealth Bomber

Signature reduction at strategic scale.

The B-2 is one of the clearest examples of stealth shaping becoming the aircraft. I'm drawn to how it blends mission-level thinking, survivability, and systems integration into one clean flying-wing solution.

Spacecraft & Exploration Systems

Launch Vehicles | Crewed Flight | Planetary Systems
Saturn VMoon Rocket

Brute force with precision.

The Saturn V is engineering at full scale. Staging decisions, clustered engines, and a mission profile that demanded reliability under enormous forces. Every part of the vehicle reflects disciplined systems engineering.

Space ShuttleReusable Orbiter

Reentry, operations, and complexity at scale.

The Shuttle combined launch vehicle, spacecraft, and runway recovery into one system. I'm drawn to that mix of operations, thermal protection, and the compromises that made reusable crewed flight work.

Falcon 9Reusable Booster

Control, iteration, and landing precision.

Falcon 9 stands out because it turned precision guidance, telemetry, and closed-loop control into a repeatable recovery system. It is one of the clearest examples of modern aerospace iteration done right.

StarshipHeavy Lift

Scale, ambition, and rapid iteration.

What draws me to Starship is how unapologetically ambitious the architecture is. It feels like a system designed for scale, operational reuse, and a future where aerospace hardware is built to fly often.

Apollo CSMCrew Transport

Mission architecture with no wasted margin.

The Apollo Command and Service Module represents compact, high-consequence engineering. It carried people through deep space, supported navigation and reentry, and had to work with an unforgiving level of reliability.

Lunar ModuleMoon Lander

Built only for the mission that mattered.

The Lunar Module was built for one environment and one job. No atmospheric compromise, no extra polish. That kind of purpose-built machine is what I like studying.

IngenuityMars Rotorcraft

Tiny vehicle, huge systems achievement.

Ingenuity is one of my favorite examples of aerospace engineering because it brought powered flight to another planet. It's a reminder that elegant control, autonomy, and lightweight design can still redefine what is possible.

PerseverancePlanetary Rover

Robotics where there is no second chance.

On Perseverance, every subsystem has to justify itself. Watt, gram, and code budgets are tight. Planetary robotics with no second chance to fix it on site.

Philosophy

Philosophy is a separate lane from fiction. I come back to stoic and classical ethics when I need a clear frame for judgment, effort, and what is actually under my control: in the shop, in training, and in long projects.

Marcus Aurelius

Meditations

A reminder to stay disciplined, calm, and focused on what can be controlled.

Aristotle

Nicomachean Ethics

Character as a practiced habit. Excellence comes from repeated, deliberate action, not intention alone.

Films

Film is its own interest: atmosphere, pacing, and characters under pressure. These stand out for discipline and control on screen, not as stand-ins for the books I read.

No Country for Old Men

Quiet tension and hard choices. Little spectacle, a lot of pressure.

The Shawshank Redemption

A story about patience, discipline, and long-term persistence. Even when progress feels invisible.

Taxi Driver

A character study with atmosphere and intensity. Slow-burn storytelling that builds pressure deliberately.

The Dark Knight

Tight pacing and moral pressure. Every scene feels deliberate.

The Lord of the Rings Trilogy

Three films built as one long campaign. Endurance, loyalty, and a small team carrying a mission no one else could finish.

Science Fiction

Science fiction is where I go for systems under hard limits: colonies, orbital mechanics, improvisation, and crews that have to make sparse resources work. Separate from philosophy and from general reading.

Robert A. Heinlein

The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress

An engineer, a self-aware computer, and a lunar colony. Resource limits, orbital mechanics, and planning against long odds.

Robert A. Heinlein

Farmer in the Sky

Building a working colony on Ganymede from almost nothing. Hard work, improvisation, and systems that have to keep people alive.

Reading

Reading covers the rest of the shelf: aerospace history and fundamentals, plus long-form fiction that rewards endurance. Not philosophy, not sci-fi. Just the broader habit of sitting with a book.

Ben R. Rich & Leo Janos

Skunk Works

Engineering culture, secrecy, risk, and aircraft development under extreme constraints.

Doug Richardson

Stealth Warplanes

A technical look at stealth aircraft, signature reduction, and modern airpower design.

John D. Anderson

Introduction to Flight

A foundation for understanding flight mechanics, aerodynamics, propulsion, and aircraft performance.

J.R.R. Tolkien

The Fellowship of the Ring

A team assembled from people who have no reason to work together, committed to a mission bigger than any of them.

J.R.R. Tolkien

The Two Towers

The team splits and every thread has to hold on its own. Progress made without knowing how the other fronts are going.

J.R.R. Tolkien

The Return of the King

Seeing the mission through to the end. The last stretch is the hardest, and it only works because nobody quit earlier.
