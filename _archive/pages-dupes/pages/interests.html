Aerospace & Flight

I’ve always looked at aircraft, spacecraft, and launch vehicles the same way I look at complex engineering systems: reduce them down to fundamentals, understand the constraints, and study why each design choice exists. These platforms stand out because they reward deep curiosity and disciplined analysis. They are engineering pushed toward the limit.

Aircraft

Fighters · Recon · Bombers
F-117 Nighthawk Stealth Attack

First in line for pure stealth engineering.

The F-117 stands out to me because it feels like a machine built around one hard constraint: survivability through signature reduction. It reflects a mindset I respect deeply. Accept aerodynamic tradeoffs, then use systems and mission design to make the aircraft effective anyway.

SR-71 Blackbird Recon · Mach 3+

Outrun the threat instead of dodging it.

The SR-71 is a perfect example of engineering pushed into the extreme. I’m drawn to how its design handles continuous heating, material expansion, and mission reliability at Mach 3+, where every decision has direct consequences for survivability and performance.

F-22 Raptor 5th Gen Fighter

Stealth, supercruise, and control laws.

The F-22 has always stood out to me for how seamlessly aerodynamics, avionics, and control laws work together. It’s a system where real-time software, stealth geometry, and thrust vectoring feel integrated rather than layered on top of each other.

YF-23 “Black Widow II” Prototype

The stealthy “what if” path.

I’ve always been fascinated by the YF-23 because it represents a completely different path the ATF program could have taken. Its emphasis on signature reduction, high-speed stability, and a unique planform shows how different engineering priorities can shape an entirely separate solution to the same problem.

F-14 Tomcat Fleet Defense Fighter

Variable geometry with attitude.

The F-14 stands out because it feels mechanically alive. Between the variable-sweep wings, naval mission demands, and long-range intercept role, it represents an era where geometry, propulsion, and mission profile all had to work together in a very visible way.

F-16 “Viper” FBW Pioneer

Relaxed stability as a feature.

The F-16’s intentionally unstable design has always been one of my favorite examples of control theory in action. It shows how software, real-time feedback, and control surface authority can turn instability into agility.

B-2 Spirit Stealth Bomber

Signature reduction at strategic scale.

The B-2 is one of the clearest examples of stealth shaping becoming the aircraft. I’m drawn to how it blends mission-level thinking, survivability, and systems integration into one clean flying-wing solution.

B-52 Stratofortress Strategic Bomber

Endurance, payload, and staying power.

The B-52 earns its place because it proves that a platform can stay relevant for decades when the architecture is adaptable. It’s less about novelty and more about durability, payload, and mission usefulness over time.

Spacecraft & Exploration Systems

Launch Vehicles · Crewed Flight · Planetary Systems
Saturn V Moon Rocket

Brute force with precision.

The Saturn V is engineering at full scale. Staging decisions, clustered engines, and a mission profile that demanded reliability under enormous forces. Every part of the vehicle reflects disciplined systems engineering.

Space Shuttle Reusable Orbiter

Reentry, operations, and complexity at scale.

The Shuttle is fascinating because it combined launch vehicle, spacecraft, and glider-like recovery into a single system. It feels like a masterclass in compromise, operations, and ambitious engineering.

Falcon 9 Reusable Booster

Control, iteration, and landing precision.

Falcon 9 stands out because it turned precision guidance, telemetry, and closed-loop control into a repeatable recovery system. It is one of the clearest examples of modern aerospace iteration done right.

Starship Heavy Lift

Scale, ambition, and rapid iteration.

What draws me to Starship is how unapologetically ambitious the architecture is. It feels like a system designed for scale, operational reuse, and a future where aerospace hardware is built to fly often.

Apollo CSM Crew Transport

Mission architecture with no wasted margin.

The Apollo Command and Service Module represents compact, high-consequence engineering. It carried people through deep space, supported navigation and reentry, and had to work with an unforgiving level of reliability.

Lunar Module Moon Lander

Built only for the mission that mattered.

I love the Lunar Module because it feels brutally honest. No atmospheric compromise, no unnecessary elegance, just a machine optimized for one environment and one purpose. That kind of engineering discipline is beautiful.

Ingenuity Mars Rotorcraft

Tiny vehicle, huge systems achievement.

Ingenuity is one of my favorite examples of aerospace engineering because it brought powered flight to another planet. It’s a reminder that elegant control, autonomy, and lightweight design can still redefine what is possible.

Perseverance Planetary Rover

Robotics where there is no second chance.

Perseverance represents the kind of robotics I admire most. Every subsystem has to justify itself. Every watt, gram, and line of code matters. It’s exploration through careful, high-stakes systems engineering.

Favorite Real-World Missions

These missions shaped how I think about engineering under pressure: making decisions with incomplete data, building systems that tolerate failure, and staying calm when the margin for error is effectively zero. They’re real examples of control, discipline, and teamwork at scale.

Apollo 13

Real-time systems engineering: reconfiguring hardware, procedures, and checklists on the fly to bring a damaged spacecraft home safely.

Falcon 9 First Landing

Closed-loop control, iteration, and telemetry-driven decision-making—turning something that looked impossible into a repeatable, reliable system.

Perseverance & Ingenuity

Precision robotics and control in an environment you can't touch—every gram, watt, and line of code has to earn its place.

Counter-UAS Field Exercise

Sensor fusion, autonomy, and fast decision loops—treating the airspace as a dynamic system that has to be understood, not just observed.

Training & Movement

Training has always felt similar to engineering work for me—set a clear target, define the constraints, and apply consistent effort. I’m drawn to simple movements performed with high intent, where progress comes from discipline rather than tricks.

Calisthenics

Weighted pull-ups & dips

Weighted pull-ups and dips have become my primary strength movements. I focus on controlled execution, progressive overload, and keeping the work honest—no shortcuts, no wasted reps.

Style Heavy, clean, controlled
Why Simple movements, high intent

Strength Training

No reps left in reserve

Most of my working sets are done at a high effort level—usually 0–1 RIR. It feels similar to tuning a control loop: push hard, observe the response, adjust, and build resilience over time. The structure and discipline carry over directly into engineering work.

Philosophy Make top sets memorable
Carryover Same mindset as debugging & EKF tuning
